Isolating Caffeine from Tea Leaves

2026-11 Lab notes C₈H₁₀N₄O₂

Caffeine (1,3,7-trimethylxanthine) is a purine alkaloid that makes up roughly 2–4% of dry tea leaves. Isolating it is a classic organic chemistry experiment because it combines several core lab techniques in one afternoon.

First, the tea leaves are boiled in water with sodium carbonate. The base converts tannins, the acidic compounds behind tea's astringent taste, into water-soluble salts, while caffeine stays in its neutral, free-base form. After cooling, the brew is extracted with dichloromethane in a separatory funnel. Caffeine is far more soluble in the organic layer, so it moves out of the water. Gentle swirling instead of hard shaking helps avoid stubborn emulsions.

The organic layer is then dried with anhydrous sodium sulfate and evaporated, leaving crude, greenish caffeine. Finally, sublimation purifies it: caffeine passes directly from solid to vapor and condenses as fine, white, needle-like crystals on a cold surface.

In short

  1. Boil tea with Na₂CO₃ in water
  2. Extract with CH₂Cl₂ in a separatory funnel
  3. Dry over anhydrous Na₂SO₄ and evaporate
  4. Purify the crude caffeine by sublimation
